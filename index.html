About the Program

What is the NSF GRFP?

The NSF Graduate Research Fellowship Program has invested in outstanding graduate students in STEM since 1952 — making it the nation's oldest graduate fellowship program. It funds the person, not the project. NSF is betting on your long-term potential as a scientist, not a single research question. With 70,000+ fellows over 73 years, the network of GRFP alumni includes 30 Nobel Laureates, 400+ National Academy members, and founders of major companies.

The competitive reality: 12,000–17,000 students apply each year, and roughly 2,000 are selected. You must package yourself well. According to Claire McKay Bowen, a 2015 GRFP fellow who reviewed hundreds of applications at Notre Dame, winners are either "golden" people who cannot fail — or people who know how to present themselves compellingly. This guide focuses on the second group.

$53K / yr

$37,000 stipend to you + $16,000 to your institution covering all tuition and mandatory fees — no cost to you.

3 yrs support

Three funded years over a five-year fellowship window. You may defer up to two years if you have another funding source.

No service req.

No post-graduate service requirement and no restrictions on your PI's grant. Freedom to pursue your research.

73 years strong

70K+ fellowships at 500+ institutions. Alumni include 30 Nobel laureates. The prestige and network are real and lasting.

What You Submit

Application Components

Two essays plus letters of reference — all evaluated against Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts. Follow the guidelines exactly; reviewers screen for missing components and formatting violations first.

Personal Statement, Relevant Background & Future Goals

3 pages

Your story: who you are, what shaped your path to science, your research experience, and where you're going. NSF wants to invest in you — not just a research question. Make reviewers emotionally invested in your journey.

  • Personal Statement — your origin story~1 page
  • Relevant Background — your research experiences~1 page
  • Future Goals — your career vision~½ page
  • Intellectual Merit — explicit header required~¼ page
  • Broader Impacts — explicit header required~¼ page

Graduate Research Plan Statement

2 pages

An original research proposal in your NSF field. You aren't contractually bound to this project — it demonstrates you can think like a scientist. A common mistake: treating it like a standalone grant rather than connecting it to your personal statement.

  • Introduction — problem, past work, why it matters~⅓ page
  • Objective — clear statement of what you will doconcise
  • Methodology — plan, contingencies, your skills~½ page
  • Anticipated Results & Productsbrief
  • Intellectual Merit — explicit header required~⅓ page
  • Broader Impacts — explicit header required~⅓ page

📬 Letters of Reference — Ask 4 to Guarantee 3 Submit on Time

The solicitation specifies the minimum number of letters required — verify the exact count in the current solicitation, as requirements can change year to year. Regardless of the maximum allowed, request more writers than the minimum so you are protected if one misses the deadline. Many applications are disqualified for missing letters. Speak to letter writers at least 2 months in advance and provide your draft essays so they can complement — not repeat — what you say.

Letter 1 — Intellectual MeritCurrent graduate advisor or potential advisor who can speak to your research ability, independence, and potential to complete graduate school.
Letter 2 — Intellectual MeritPast research advisor, lab PI, or internship supervisor who has seen your hands-on scientific work in depth.
Letter 3 — Broader ImpactsA mentor from teaching, outreach, or service — someone who can speak to your impact on others outside the lab. This is the one most applicants neglect.

What reviewers actually write about letters — quotes from real review panels

  • She has strong letters praising her independence, ambition, breadth and depth of knowledge, and passion to produce promising and valuable research.
  • Strong recommendations from three different peers at R1 universities in the nation and around the world give confidence that this applicant has a bright future in her chosen field.
  • The applicant is well-rounded and has been described as being friendly and working well with others — all references give their highest recommendations.
  • Applicant's great work ethic as attested to by references as being more than that of other graduate students is an acknowledgement of demonstrated initiative and potential for graduate work.
How You're Scored

The Two Review Criteria

Every component — both essays and all letters — must address both criteria. Both require explicit section headers in each essay. Reviewers score independently, then discuss applications near cut-offs.

⚗️ Intellectual Merit

"The potential to advance knowledge and understanding within its own field or across different fields."

  • How does your work advance knowledge in your field or across fields?
  • What creative or potentially transformative concepts are in play?
  • Is your research plan well-reasoned and organized, with a mechanism to assess success?
  • What are your qualifications, skills, and relevant experience?
  • Do you have access to adequate resources — mentors, equipment, collaborators?
🌎 Broader Impacts

"The potential to benefit society and contribute to desired societal outcomes." — The make-or-break criterion. Don't save it for last.

  • Full participation of underrepresented groups in STEM
  • Improved STEM education and educator development at any level
  • Increased public scientific literacy and community engagement
  • Development of a diverse, globally competitive STEM workforce
  • Improved well-being of individuals in society
  • Health equity and reduction of health disparities

Intellectual Merit — 5 sub-criteria reviewers evaluate (BI is scored separately on its own criteria)

①Scientific or societal impact of the research question
②Why fund you — your unique innovation and perspective
③Detailed plan with built-in success metrics
④Your qualifications, skills, and research experience
⑤Resources, mentors, and collaborators to execute
Writing Strategies

The Essay Writing Guide

Five tabs covering every dimension of the application — from structure to sentence-level craft. Informed by Alex Lang, Logan Pearce, and Claire McKay Bowen.

Recommended structure

  1. Personal Statement~1 page — Your origin story: why science? What unique perspective do you bring? Include hardships overcome, communities you belong to, what makes you distinctively you. NSF funds the person — make reviewers care about you personally.
  2. Relevant Background~1 page — Research experiences, one paragraph each: why you chose that lab, the science goal, your specific contribution, what you produced, results, how you shared the work, and what you learned. Quantify everything you can.
  3. Future Goals~½ page — Tie your background and mission into a coherent vision. Where are you going, why does it matter, and how will NSF support help you get there?
  4. Intellectual Meritexplicit header — Concise summary of how your past and planned work advances knowledge in your field.
  5. Broader Impactsexplicit header — Concise summary of your contributions to society, STEM diversity, and community. In this essay, BI is about you — your teaching, mentoring, outreach, and community-building — not just your research topic.

Key tips

  • Both essays need explicit IM and BI section headers — this is required, not optional. Missing headers is a disqualifying signal to reviewers.
  • Tell a story, not a CV. Think of a thread that ties all your experiences into a coherent narrative. Where did you start? What shaped you? Where are you going? Make reviewers emotionally invested.
  • For each research experience, follow the 7-element formula: why you chose that lab → science goal → your specific role → what you produced → results → how you shared it → what you learned. Be specific — "I designed experiments" beats "I helped with research."
  • If you faced adversity (first-gen student, financial hardship, caretaker role), include it — NSF values the full participation of underrepresented groups and wants to understand what shaped your path.
  • Address any weaknesses in your record — a GPA dip, a field change, a gap year — without stating them as problems. Show what you gained and why it makes you stronger.
  • Avoid "I hope," "I believe," "I think." Use "I will," "I intend to," "I plan to." Confidence is a review criterion. Own your accomplishments.
  • Do not say you were "lucky" or "fortunate" to have opportunities. You pursued them. State it that way. (See Writing Craft tab for before/after examples.)
  • Write for a general science audience. Your reviewer is in your primary field but not your subfield — minimize jargon or define it clearly when used.

Recommended structure (Claire McKay Bowen's outline)

  1. Introduction — Problem, past attempts, and additional issues. This should be your largest section — bring the reader up to speed on scientific context. Include several citations. Grab them with the first paragraph: what is at stake and why should anyone care?
  2. Objective — A clear, concise statement of what you will do. One to three sentences. Consider bolding this sentence — make it unmissable.
  3. Methodology — Model development, evaluation, software, and other approaches. Show major steps, your creative angle, contingency plans for high-risk steps, and why you specifically are equipped to execute this.
  4. Anticipated Results and Products — What does success look like? What will you produce? What would a "failed" experiment still teach you? This is a specific review criterion — include it explicitly.
  5. Intellectual Meritexplicit header — Address all five review criteria: impact, your unique innovation, plan quality, your qualifications, and your resources.
  6. Broader Impactsexplicit header — How does this research benefit society locally, nationally, and globally? How will you disseminate results? Do not treat this as an afterthought.
  7. References — No required format; can use 10pt font. Include citations in the intro to establish the current state of research.

Key tips

  • Do not treat this as a standalone grant proposal. It must connect to your personal statement — the research question should feel like a natural extension of your journey, not a separate document.
  • NSF funds the person, not the project. You're not contractually bound to this research — but you must demonstrate you think and write like a scientist.
  • The introduction should be the largest section. Don't assume any domain-specific knowledge. Define key terms. Explain why the problem matters before proposing your solution.
  • Identify the novel element of your approach. What has been done before, and what gap does your work fill? Use words like "crucial," "critical," or "transformative" — but earn them with evidence.
  • Include a mechanism to assess success. What does "it worked" look like? This is an explicit review criterion. Don't skip it.
  • Include at least one alternative approach for a high-risk step. This signals scientific maturity and careful thinking.
  • Consider including a figure — a diagram, schematic, or data chart. It can efficiently communicate complex ideas and make your proposal memorable.
  • For SACS students: topics like AI/ML for health disparities, federated learning for privacy-preserving healthcare, bioinformatics pipelines, or cybersecurity for healthcare data map strongly to both IM and BI.

Writing makeovers — before and after (from Logan Pearce's GRFP consultant guide)

The most common writing weaknesses in GRFP applications. Each pair below shows the problem and the fix. Apply these throughout both essays.

1. Active voice — identify who is doing the action
✗ Before
This data analysis pipeline will be used to perform analysis of black hole spectra.
✓ After (active voice)
I will use this data analysis pipeline to analyze black hole spectra. (In GRFP, always prefer "I" over "we" — see Strategic Bolding tab.)
✗ Before
I became familiar with the Python package.
✓ After
I familiarized myself with the Python package. / I learned to use this Python package.
✗ Before
I assisted with the project.
✓ After
I contributed to the project. / I led the data collection phase of the project.
2. Strong declarative language — commit to your future
✗ Before
I hope to obtain a position as faculty in geosciences.
✓ After
I intend to pursue a career as faculty in geosciences.
✗ Before
I would like to start a STEM outreach program for high schoolers.
✓ After
I plan to launch a STEM outreach program targeting underrepresented high school students in Nashville.
3. Don't minimize yourself — own your opportunities
✗ Before
I was privileged to attend the summer research institute.
✓ After
I attended the summer research institute. / I sought out and was selected for the summer research institute.
✗ Before
I was fortunate that Professor X took me on as a researcher.
✓ After
I approached Professor X about a research project and began working in her lab that summer.
4. Replace "passion" — show, don't label
✗ Before
I am passionate about STEM outreach to young women in high school.
✓ After
Because of my experience as a woman in STEM, I understand first-hand the impact of women-focused outreach — and I have been building that for others ever since.
✗ Before
My passion for data science drives my research into federated learning.
✓ After
Three years of building privacy-preserving systems for clinical data showed me exactly why federated learning matters — and where the field still falls short.

Opening paragraph do's and don'ts

  • Don't start with childhood stargazing or your grandparent's illness — by application 80 of 100, these stories all sound the same. Reviewers lose interest fast.
  • Instead: a strong declaration of career intent, a specific research moment that changed your direction, or something genuinely unexpected about your path to science.
  • Consider starting with what you will do, then stepping back to explain how you got here. This immediately positions you as a scientist with purpose.
  • What is unique about you and your journey? Turn what could sound like a disadvantage into a demonstrated strength. "Taking years off between undergrad and grad school gave me perspective I couldn't have gotten otherwise."
  • After you write your opener, ask: "Have 80% of applicants written something similar?" If yes, rewrite.

Strategic bolding & audience awareness

  • Use "I", "me", "my" as much as possible — not "we" and "our." This is your application, not your lab's. Take ownership of your work. "I designed, I conducted, I wrote" — not "we conducted."
  • Bold selectively — one or two sentences per essay, maximum. Consider bolding your career goal statement and your central research proposal sentence. Too much bolding dilutes impact.
  • Write for your primary field, not your subfield. Your reviewer has a PhD in your field but may not know your specific niche — define key terms if they're essential to your proposal.
  • Connect the dots for your reader explicitly. Don't assume they'll draw the inference you want from two pieces of evidence — tell them what you want them to conclude.
  • Ask non-scientists to read your essays. Colleagues, family, department admins — if they can't follow it, your subfield jargon is too heavy. Revise.

Logan Pearce, who spent two years as a GRFP consultant, calls Broader Impacts the "make-or-break" section. It's what most applicants save for last — and what most applicants write weakly. Don't. A strong BI section has five qualities.

1

Connect to your story

By the time the reader reaches your BI section, they should already be primed. Your outreach plans should flow naturally from what you've told them about yourself. Example: "Because I attended a rural school with no STEM enrichment, I understand exactly why that gap exists — and I intend to close it for others."

2

Propose something specific for the future

Don't just describe what you've done — describe what you will do. Expand a program you run, contribute to an existing initiative, or propose something new. Reviewers want to see that you have a concrete plan, not a vague aspiration.

3

Be specific — not vague

"I want to support women in STEM" is weak. "I will run for president of the Women in STEM Council and expand our after-school programming to three underserved Nashville middle schools, targeting teachers at Title I schools" is strong. Name specific populations, programs, and timelines.

4

Connect Personal Statement BI to Research Statement BI

When possible, let the two BI sections reinforce each other. If your personal statement describes mentoring Black students in STEM, your research proposal's BI might discuss training diverse lab members or publishing open-access tools for under-resourced institutions.

5

Ground it in numbers and evidence

Numbers make BI claims credible. "Attendance grew from 20 to 85 participants over two years" is far stronger than "participation increased." "I mentored 6 undergrad researchers, 4 of whom are now in PhD programs" is stronger than "I mentored undergraduates." Wherever you have numbers, use them.

NSF explicitly values: "full participation of women, persons with disabilities, and underrepresented minorities in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics." Also: creating a teaching curriculum to improve STEM at a local school; developing STEM programs specifically targeting minorities; organizing events for undergraduate or graduate students. Helping a single student does not count as Broader Impact — systemic, scalable, or program-level work does.

From Claire McKay Bowen's review of hundreds of applications and Logan Pearce's two years of GRFP consulting — the patterns that consistently weaken otherwise strong applications.

Pitfall 1

NSF funds YOU — but your language says otherwise

Using "we/our" instead of "I/my/me", saying "I hope" instead of "I will", minimizing your role with "I assisted" or "I helped." Every sentence should paint you as the driving force of your science.

  • Replace "we conducted" with "I led"
  • Replace "I hope to become" with "I will pursue"
  • Replace "I was able to" with the actual verb ("I attended")
Pitfall 2

No coherent story thread

A list of experiences without a narrative through-line. Reviewers read 15–25 applications in a stack — memorable ones have a clear arc from background to present to future.

  • Outline your story chronologically before writing
  • Ask: "What is the one thing I want reviewers to remember about me?"
  • Make sure each experience connects to the next
Pitfall 3

Weak Broader Impacts — saved for last, written in five minutes

The most common reason for a mediocre score. BI is what differentiates equally-qualified applicants. Generic statements like "I will mentor students" without specifics are red flags.

  • Write BI early — not at the end of the process
  • Name specific programs, populations, timelines
  • Ground claims in numbers wherever possible
Pitfall 4

Not addressing weaknesses in your record

A GPA dip, a field change, missing publications for your level of experience — reviewers will notice. Silence on these is worse than addressing them. Turn them into strengths without naming them as deficits.

  • Don't say "I have a low GPA, but..." — just demonstrate capability
  • Show what you gained from non-linear paths
  • Let your research accomplishments speak louder than transcripts
Pitfall 5

Missing application components or formatting violations

Reviewers screen for compliance first. Missing letters, exceeded page limits, wrong font size, or missing IM/BI explicit headers can disqualify an otherwise excellent application before it's read.

  • Submit at least 48 hours early
  • Confirm all letters are submitted (not just requested)
  • Check font size, margins, and page limits against the solicitation
Pitfall 6

Cliché openings and overused phrases

"When I was a child I became interested in science…" "I am passionate about…" By application 50 of 100, these openers all sound alike. Strong openings are specific, distinctive, and show — not tell.

  • Avoid the "passion" word entirely — demonstrate it instead
  • Avoid childhood stargazing and grandparent illness stories
  • Start with something specific to you
